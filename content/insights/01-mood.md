## 1 · why-anxiety-comes-out-of-nowhere
**Why the anxiety comes out of nowhere**

Estrogen doesn't decline smoothly. It swings — sometimes higher than it ever was in your twenties, then crashes. That's why the anxiety comes out of nowhere.

Estrogen helps regulate serotonin. When it drops fast, serotonin signalling drops with it, and the brain reads that as threat. Progesterone, the calming hormone, falls first and falls further. The result is an alarm system with less holding it back.

This is why anxiety often arrives before the hot flashes, and why it so often gets treated on its own. It is an endocrine event with a psychological signature. Watch for anxiety that climbs in the week before your period and eases once bleeding starts. 2 cycles of logging is enough to see it.

## 2 · progesterone-goes-first
**Progesterone, and why it goes first**

Progesterone is made after you ovulate. In perimenopause, you stop ovulating every month — so in those cycles, there is almost no progesterone at all.

That matters because progesterone breaks down into a compound called allopregnanolone, which acts on the same brain receptors as anti-anxiety medication. It is part of what has kept you steady, and you never knew it was there. Estrogen can still be normal, or high, while this is happening. A single blood test often shows nothing wrong.

The first signs are usually sleep that turns light, a shorter fuse, and periods that come closer together. If your cycle has shortened by a few days and your nights have changed at the same time, that is worth logging — and worth saying out loud.

## 3 · rage-is-not-your-character
**The rage is not a character flaw**

Irritability is one of the most common mood symptoms of perimenopause, and one of the least talked about. The sudden anger at a dishwasher loaded wrong has a mechanism.

Estrogen supports serotonin, which is part of what puts space between feeling something and reacting to it. When estrogen swings, that space narrows. Add broken sleep — which on its own makes the brain's threat centre more reactive and its brake weaker — and small things land like big ones.

It does not mean you have become someone else. It means the system that used to absorb frustration has less to work with. Log it when it happens, even if it feels petty. Rage that clusters around your cycle, or after bad nights, is a pattern a doctor can act on.

## 4 · first-panic-attack-in-your-forties
**A first panic attack in your forties**

If you had your first panic attack after 40, with no history of them, look at your hormones before you look at your past.

A hot flash is a sudden surge of the body's alarm response: heart rate jumps, heat floods in, the skin prickles. The brain can read those same sensations as danger and escalate them into panic — sometimes with no heat you notice at all. Falling progesterone removes some of the calm that would have stopped it.

That is why a racing heart at night, or a wave of dread just before a flush, is so common now. It is also why it gets missed. Log palpitations and panic separately, with the time of day. If they arrive together, or at night, that is information your doctor needs.

## 5 · low-mood-or-depression
**Low mood, and when it is more than that**

The risk of depression rises during perimenopause — including for women who have never had it before. This is one of the best-established findings in midlife research.

Estrogen shapes how serotonin, dopamine and noradrenaline work. When it becomes unpredictable, mood can flatten for days at a time, then lift for no reason you can name. Poor sleep and night sweats pile on top. Low mood that comes and goes with your cycle is common. Low mood that stays for 2 weeks or more, where nothing feels worth doing, is depression, and it is treatable.

Log it honestly, including the flat days. If you ever find yourself thinking that you'd be better off gone, that is urgent. Tell someone today, and call your doctor or a crisis line.

## 6 · loss-of-motivation-and-dopamine
**Why you can't make yourself start**

Loss of motivation is not laziness. Estrogen supports dopamine, the brain chemical that makes you want to start things and feel rewarded when you finish them.

When estrogen falls, the pull toward things you used to enjoy weakens. The task hasn't changed — the signal that says "this is worth it" has got quieter. Fatigue and broken sleep make it quieter still. Many women describe it as flatness rather than sadness: nothing is wrong, and nothing is pulling you forward either.

This is easy to mistake for burnout, and it is often treated as that. Log the days it's there and the days it isn't. If it tracks your cycle, or your worst nights, that points to a hormonal cause — not a personal one.

## 7 · the-week-before-your-period
**Why the week before your period got worse**

Premenstrual symptoms often get worse in perimenopause, not better. The week before your period can start to feel like a different person's week.

In the second half of a normal cycle, estrogen and progesterone rise and then fall. In perimenopause, that fall is steeper and less predictable, and some cycles have no progesterone rise at all. The brain is reacting to the size and speed of the change, not the level. That is why mood can crash while blood tests look normal.

Log mood alongside your period days. If the worst days sit in the 7 to 10 days before bleeding, month after month, that is a pattern with a name. Showing it to a doctor changes the conversation from "you seem stressed" to "this is cyclical".

## 8 · the-sleep-mood-domino
**The night sweats are also a mood symptom**

Some of what feels like a mood problem starts at night. Researchers call it the domino effect: the night sweat wakes you, and the broken sleep knocks over the next day.

One bad night makes the brain's threat centre more reactive and the planning part of the brain less able to calm it. A run of them looks a lot like anxiety or depression. You may not even remember waking. You just know the day feels harder than it should.

This is why treating night sweats can lift mood, and why mood gets blamed on everything but sleep. Log both on the same day. If your worst mood days follow your worst nights, that connection is the single most useful thing you can show your doctor.

## 9 · it-might-not-just-be-stress
**It might not just be stress**

You are in your late thirties or your forties. There is probably work, children, parents, money. So when anxiety or low mood arrives, everyone — including you — reaches for stress as the explanation.

Stress is real, and it is also the easiest way to miss perimenopause. Life stress doesn't follow a 3 to 5 week rhythm. Hormones do. Stress doesn't usually bring night sweats, heavier periods, or a heartbeat you can feel. When mood symptoms arrive alongside physical ones, and move with your cycle, the cause is more than your calendar.

Both can be true at once, and that is the point. You don't have to prove your life is calm to be taken seriously. Your log shows the timing. The timing is what separates the two.

## 10 · your-history-is-data
**Your history is data**

Some women are more sensitive to hormonal change than others, and the signs usually showed up years ago. If you had bad PMS, depression after a birth, or low mood on the pill, you are more likely to feel perimenopause in your mood.

It isn't the amount of hormone that differs. It's how strongly the brain reacts when levels move. That sensitivity tends to be consistent across a lifetime. The same brain that reacted to the drop after childbirth is reacting to the swings now.

This is worth telling your doctor directly, because it's rarely asked. Say it as history, in one sentence: "I had postnatal depression, and my PMS was always bad." It moves perimenopause up the list of explanations, where it belongs.

## 11 · why-small-things-feel-enormous
**Why small things feel enormous**

Crying at an advert. Feeling crushed by a slightly short email. Emotional volatility is a recognised perimenopause symptom, and it has the same roots as the anxiety and the irritability.

Estrogen and progesterone both help regulate how strongly you respond to emotional input and how quickly you recover. When they swing, responses get bigger and take longer to settle. Tiredness makes the effect larger. None of this is about how much the thing matters. It's about how much buffer the brain has left.

It can be disorienting when you have always been the steady one. Log it as mood, even when it seems too small to count. The size of the reaction compared with the trigger is exactly the detail that makes the pattern clear.

## 12 · anxiety-at-4am
**Why the worry is worst at 4am**

Waking before dawn with your mind already racing has a clock behind it. Cortisol, the body's alertness hormone, starts rising in the early hours to get you ready for morning.

In perimenopause, falling estrogen and progesterone make sleep lighter in exactly that stretch of the night, and a night sweat can tip you fully awake. You surface into rising cortisol, darkness, and nothing to do but think. The brain's worry circuits are running with the planning part still half asleep. Problems feel larger at 4am because the part of you that would put them in proportion isn't fully online.

Log the time you woke, not just that you did. A pattern of early waking followed by anxiety connects the mood to the sleep, and gives you something specific to treat.

## 13 · wine-hits-differently
**Why one glass of wine hits differently now**

If 1 or 2 glasses leave you anxious the next day, sweating in the night, or awake at 3am, that is a common change in perimenopause — not an imagined one.

Alcohol calms the brain at first, acting on the same receptors progesterone used to support. As it wears off, the brain rebounds into a more alert, anxious state — usually in the middle of the night. Alcohol also widens blood vessels, which can trigger hot flashes and night sweats. With less progesterone to cushion the rebound, the swing is bigger than it used to be.

You don't have to give anything up to find out if this is you. Log the nights you drank and look at the day after. Your own data answers the question better than a rule.

## 14 · what-helps-perimenopausal-mood
**What actually helps with perimenopausal mood**

Mood symptoms in perimenopause respond to treatment. You don't have to wait them out, and they are not something to push through.

The options sit in 3 groups. Hormone therapy can steady the swings driving the symptoms, and has evidence for mood specifically during perimenopause. Antidepressants help many women and can also reduce hot flashes. Cognitive behavioural therapy has good evidence for anxiety and for how much the physical symptoms bother you. Exercise and treating poor sleep help with all of it. They are not either-or.

Which one fits depends on you, your history and your symptoms. That is the conversation to have, and your log is how you start it. Bring the pattern, not only the worst day.

## 15 · it-has-an-end-point
**It has an end point**

Perimenopause is a transition, not a permanent change in who you are. For most women, the mood symptoms driven by hormone swings ease in the years after the final period, once levels stop moving.

The problem was never the low level of estrogen on its own. It is the instability — the rises and crashes. When the swings stop, the brain adapts to the new, steady baseline. That is why the risk of depression that rises during perimenopause tends to fall again afterward.

That doesn't mean waiting. The transition can last several years, and treatment makes those years better. But knowing it has a shape matters. What you are logging is not a new personality. It is a phase with a mechanism, and you can see it.
