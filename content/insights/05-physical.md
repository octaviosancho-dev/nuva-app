# Insights — batch 5: physical

Category: `physical`. 15 insights. Three paragraphs each, never more.

---

## 1 · why-it-all-happens-at-once
**Why it all seems to happen at once**

Aching joints, a racing heart, headaches, bloating, exhaustion — at the same time as the mood and the sleep. It looks like a list of unrelated problems. It has one cause.

Estrogen receptors are found throughout the body: in the brain, the heart and blood vessels, the joints, the bones, the skin, the bladder and the gut. When estrogen becomes unstable, all of those systems feel it at once. That's why perimenopause can produce symptoms in places that seem to have nothing to do with hormones.

It is also why it gets missed. Each specialist sees one piece — the joint pain, the palpitations, the headaches. Your log sees all of them together, and together is what makes the pattern obvious.

## 2 · fatigue-sleep-doesnt-fix
**Tiredness that sleep doesn't fix**

Fatigue in perimenopause is often described as a heaviness that a good night doesn't touch. It usually has more than one cause, and they add up.

Broken sleep is the biggest, even when you don't remember waking. Hormone swings affect energy directly. Heavy periods can drain your iron, and low iron makes you tired and breathless long before it shows up as anaemia. An underactive thyroid, common at this age, causes the same heavy tiredness.

If you're exhausted most days, ask for your ferritin, which measures stored iron, and your thyroid to be checked. They're simple blood tests. Log your energy daily alongside your sleep and your periods. Fatigue that follows heavy bleeding or bad nights points you straight at what to treat.

## 3 · palpitations
**When you can feel your heart**

A pounding, fluttering or racing heart, often out of nowhere, is a common symptom of perimenopause. It can be frightening, and it is usually harmless.

Estrogen affects how the heart's rhythm is regulated and how blood vessels respond. As it swings, the heart can beat faster or with extra beats you can feel. Palpitations often come with hot flashes, at night, or in the days around your period. Anxiety, caffeine and alcohol can all make them more noticeable.

Log when they happen and what came with them. Mention them to a doctor, who may check your heart rhythm, thyroid and iron. But if palpitations come with chest pain, fainting, or severe breathlessness, that is an emergency — call for help straight away.

## 4 · joint-pain
**Why your joints ache**

Aching, stiff joints — hands, shoulders, knees, hips — are one of the most common physical symptoms of perimenopause, and one of the least often linked to it.

Estrogen has an anti-inflammatory effect and helps keep joint tissues and the cartilage cushioning them in good condition. As it falls, joints can become stiffer and more painful, often worst in the morning. Many women are told it's age, or arthritis, or they've overdone it at the gym.

It can be all of those. But if joint pain arrived alongside other perimenopause symptoms, it is worth mentioning in the same breath, not in a separate appointment. Log it with where it hurts. Joint pain that eases when other symptoms are treated is a good sign of what was driving it.

## 5 · headaches-and-migraine
**Why headaches changed**

If you get migraines or headaches, perimenopause often makes them worse, more frequent, or harder to predict. Falling estrogen is a well-known migraine trigger.

Many women have had headaches around their period for years — that is the drop in estrogen before bleeding. In perimenopause, estrogen drops more often and less predictably, so the headaches follow. For many women, migraine improves once the transition ends and levels stop moving.

Log your headaches with your cycle days to see if they follow the drops. That pattern can change which treatments your doctor suggests. But a sudden, severe headache — the worst of your life — or a headache with weakness, confusion, or changes in vision or speech, is an emergency.

## 6 · dizziness
**Why you feel dizzy or light-headed**

Dizziness and light-headedness are reported by many women in perimenopause. There are several possible causes, and they are worth separating.

A hot flash can make you briefly light-headed as blood vessels open and blood pressure drops. Anxiety and fast breathing cause dizziness. Low iron from heavy periods reduces how much oxygen your blood carries. Changes in blood pressure and blood sugar can do it too. Some women notice dizziness in the days around their period.

Log when it happens and what it comes with. If it arrives with a flush, it's likely part of it. But if you faint, the room spins, or dizziness comes with chest pain or weakness, see a doctor promptly. Ask for your iron and blood pressure to be checked.

## 7 · bloating
**Why you're bloated**

Bloating that comes and goes, or a belly that feels swollen by evening, is common in perimenopause and often tracks your cycle.

High estrogen phases can make the body hold on to more water. Progesterone slows the gut, so food moves through more slowly and gas builds up. Both hormones swing more in perimenopause than they did before, so bloating becomes less predictable. Stress, sleep loss and certain foods can add to it.

Log it with your cycle and what you ate. If it follows your cycle, it's likely hormonal. But bloating that is there most days for 3 weeks or more, with feeling full quickly, pain, or changes in toilet habits, should be checked by a doctor — whatever else you're experiencing.

## 8 · weight-and-the-middle
**Why weight moves to your middle**

Many women find their weight shifts toward the waist in perimenopause, even if the scale barely moves. That is a hormonal change, not a failure of effort.

As estrogen falls, the body starts storing fat differently — less on the hips and thighs, more around the abdomen. Muscle mass also declines with age, which lowers the energy your body uses at rest. Broken sleep raises hunger and cravings. Each change is small. Together they add up.

This is why doing what always worked may stop working. The approaches with the best evidence are building and keeping muscle, protecting sleep, and adjusting eating to your current body rather than the one you had at 30. Log weight weekly, not daily. The trend matters, not the day.

## 9 · protect-your-muscle
**Muscle is the thing to protect**

From your forties onward, muscle mass declines unless you actively work to keep it. Falling estrogen speeds that up.

Muscle does more than make you strong. It uses energy at rest, helps control blood sugar, protects your joints, and keeps your bones loaded, which keeps them stronger. Losing it quietly shifts weight, energy and balance over the years.

Strength training — lifting weights, resistance bands, bodyweight exercises — is the most effective way to keep muscle, and it is effective at any age. 2 or 3 sessions a week is enough to make a difference. Eating enough protein supports it. It isn't about how you look. It's about what your body can do in 20 years.

## 10 · bone-loss
**Bone loss speeds up now**

Bone loss speeds up in the late stages of perimenopause and the first years after the final period. Estrogen is one of the main things protecting your bones, and it is leaving.

Bones are constantly broken down and rebuilt. Estrogen keeps the two in balance. When it falls, breakdown gets ahead of rebuilding, and bone density drops. There are no symptoms until a bone breaks, which is why it is easy to ignore.

What protects bone: strength training and impact exercise like brisk walking, enough calcium and vitamin D, not smoking, and limiting alcohol. Hormone therapy also prevents bone loss. If you have an early menopause, a family history of osteoporosis, or have broken a bone easily, ask about a bone density scan.

## 11 · heart-and-cholesterol
**Your heart after estrogen**

Heart disease is the leading cause of death in women, and the risk starts to climb around the menopause. It is the most important long-term change, and it gets the least attention.

Estrogen helps keep blood vessels flexible and cholesterol in a healthier balance. As it falls, LDL cholesterol — the kind linked to heart disease — tends to rise, blood pressure can creep up, and blood sugar control can worsen. These changes are silent.

Your forties are the time to know your numbers. Ask for your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar to be checked, and repeat them regularly. The things that protect your heart — movement, not smoking, sleep, and blood pressure control — have their biggest payoff when started early.

## 12 · the-symptoms-nobody-asks-about
**The symptoms nobody asks about**

Vaginal dryness, pain during sex, needing to pee more often or more urgently, and repeated urinary infections are all caused by falling estrogen. They are common, and they are rarely raised unless you raise them.

The tissues of the vagina, vulva and bladder depend on estrogen to stay thick, elastic and moist. As it falls, they become thinner and more fragile. Unlike hot flashes, these symptoms usually don't ease with time — they tend to get gradually worse. The medical name is genitourinary syndrome of menopause.

It is very treatable. Vaginal estrogen, used locally, works well and is considered safe for most women, including many who can't take other hormone therapy. If this is you, say it plainly. It is a medical symptom, not an embarrassing one.

## 13 · skin-and-hair
**Why your skin and hair changed**

Drier skin, new sensitivity, itching, thinning hair, or hair in new places — these are also hormonal. Estrogen supports collagen, skin moisture and hair growth.

As estrogen falls, collagen production drops and the skin becomes thinner, drier and less elastic. Some women feel itching or a crawling sensation on the skin with no rash. Hair on the head can thin, while the relative rise of other hormones can mean more hair on the chin or upper lip.

These are easy to dismiss as cosmetic, but they are part of the same picture. Mention them. Hair loss can also be caused by low iron or thyroid problems, which is another reason to get those checked if it's significant.

## 14 · checks-worth-asking-for
**The checks worth asking for in your forties**

Perimenopause is the moment when several silent health risks begin to change. A few simple checks give you a baseline.

Blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar tell you about heart and metabolic health. A thyroid test rules out a common cause of fatigue, weight change and mood symptoms. Ferritin shows your iron stores, which heavy periods can drain. Depending on your history, a bone density scan may be worth discussing. Keep up with cervical and breast screening.

You don't need to ask for everything at once. But don't wait to be offered. Ask which of these you're due, write down the results, and keep them. Future you will want to know where you started.

## 15 · hormone-therapy-now
**What we know about hormone therapy now**

Many women still avoid hormone therapy because of a large study published in 2002 that linked it to breast cancer and heart disease. The picture has changed a lot since then.

Later analysis of that same study showed the risks depended heavily on age and timing. Most of the women studied were in their sixties, years past menopause. For healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of their final period, the benefits for symptoms and bone generally outweigh the risks. Estrogen through the skin, as a patch or gel, does not appear to raise the risk of blood clots the way tablets do.

It isn't right for everyone, and it isn't the only option. But turning it down on the basis of old headlines is a decision worth revisiting with a doctor, with your own history in front of you.
