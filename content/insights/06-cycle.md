# Insights — batch 6: cycle

## 1 · the-first-sign-is-your-cycle
**The first sign is usually your cycle**

Before the hot flashes, often before anything you'd call a symptom, your cycle starts to change. That is typically the earliest marker of perimenopause.

Doctors define early perimenopause as cycle length changing by 7 days or more, and staying changed — a 28-day cycle that becomes 21 days, or 35. Later in the transition, gaps of 60 days or more between periods appear. Perimenopause usually begins in the mid-forties, but for many women it starts in their late thirties.

Most women don't know their cycle length well enough to notice a week's difference. Your log does. Record the first day of every period. After 3 or 4 cycles, you'll see whether yours is shifting — and that is the clearest early evidence there is.

## 2 · cycles-get-shorter-first
**Why your periods are coming closer together**

Most people expect periods to space out as menopause approaches. For many women, they get closer together first.

In early perimenopause, the ovaries respond faster to the hormone that stimulates them, and the first half of the cycle shortens. A period every 3 weeks instead of every 4 is common. Some cycles don't release an egg at all, which changes the bleeding pattern too. The spacing out usually comes later.

It's one of the most overlooked early signs, because "more frequent periods" doesn't match what most people think perimenopause looks like. If your cycle has shortened and other symptoms have arrived with it, that's a pattern worth naming — and worth logging for at least 3 months.

## 3 · flooding
**Heavy periods in perimenopause**

Periods that suddenly become much heavier — soaking through protection, clots, bleeding through clothes — are common in perimenopause. Common does not mean you have to put up with them.

In cycles where no egg is released, there's no progesterone to balance estrogen. The lining of the womb keeps building, and when it finally sheds, it sheds heavily. Fibroids and polyps, which are more common in your forties, can add to it.

Heavy means: soaking a pad or tampon every 1 to 2 hours, clots larger than a coin, bleeding for more than 7 days, or needing to plan your life around it. That needs checking, not endurance. Heavy periods are very treatable, and they can drain your iron without you realising.

## 4 · spotting
**Spotting between periods**

Light bleeding or spotting between periods happens more often in perimenopause, as hormone levels rise and fall unpredictably and the lining of the womb sheds unevenly.

A one-off episode in a cycle that has otherwise gone haywire is often hormonal. But spotting has other causes too, including polyps, infection, and changes in the cervix, and those need to be looked at.

Log every episode, even a small one, with the date. Bleeding between periods that keeps happening, bleeding after sex, or any bleeding once your periods have stopped for a year should always be checked by a doctor. Usually the cause is harmless. The point of checking is to know that, rather than assume it.

## 5 · breast-tenderness
**Why your breasts are sore**

Breast tenderness, swelling or aching can get worse in perimenopause, even if you never had it much before. It's a sign of estrogen running high, not low.

In perimenopause, estrogen doesn't only fall. In some cycles it peaks higher than it used to, especially early in the transition. Breast tissue responds to those peaks with swelling and soreness, which usually eases once your period arrives.

Log it with your cycle. Tenderness that comes and goes with your periods is typically hormonal. But a new lump, a change in the shape of a breast, skin that puckers, nipple changes or discharge, or pain in one specific spot that doesn't go away should be checked by a doctor, whatever your cycle is doing.

## 6 · cramping
**Why the cramps got worse**

Worse period pain in your forties is common, and it often goes with heavier bleeding. The more the lining builds up, the harder the womb works to shed it.

The womb contracts to shed its lining, and it uses chemicals called prostaglandins to do it. A thicker lining means more of them, and more pain. Fibroids and adenomyosis, where lining tissue grows into the muscle wall, are both more common at this age and can make pain much worse.

Pain relief taken at the start of bleeding, rather than once pain peaks, works better, because it reduces prostaglandins before they build up. But pain that stops you working, or that's new or getting worse each cycle, should be checked. An ultrasound can find the common causes.

## 7 · blood-tests-cant-confirm-it
**Why a blood test often can't confirm perimenopause**

You may have been told your hormone levels are normal, so it can't be perimenopause. That reasoning doesn't hold up.

In perimenopause, hormone levels swing from day to day and cycle to cycle. A blood test is a single snapshot. The same woman can test in the normal range one week and clearly perimenopausal the next. That's why clinical guidelines, including the UK's national guidance, say women over 45 with typical symptoms can be diagnosed from their symptoms alone, without a blood test.

Your symptoms and your cycle pattern are the evidence. A normal blood test doesn't cancel them out. If you're told otherwise, you can say: "I understand hormone levels fluctuate in perimenopause. Can we look at my symptom pattern instead?"

## 8 · you-can-still-get-pregnant
**You can still get pregnant**

Irregular periods don't mean you're not ovulating. In perimenopause, some cycles still release an egg, and pregnancy is possible until menopause is confirmed.

Because ovulation becomes unpredictable, it's harder to know when you're fertile, not easier. The general guidance is to keep using contraception until you've gone 12 months without a period if you're over 50, or 2 years if you're under 50.

Some contraception can also help with perimenopause symptoms — a hormonal coil, for example, can make heavy periods lighter and later be part of hormone therapy. If you're using contraception, it's worth asking whether yours is still the best fit for this stage.

## 9 · the-pill-can-hide-it
**Why hormonal contraception can hide perimenopause**

If you're on the combined pill, or have a hormonal coil, your cycle may not tell you much. Both can hide the early signs of perimenopause.

The combined pill replaces your own hormone cycle with a steady dose, so your bleeds are regular whatever your ovaries are doing. A hormonal coil often makes periods light or stops them altogether. In both cases, the cycle changes that usually signal perimenopause won't show up.

Other symptoms still can. Mood changes, sleep problems, brain fog, joint pain, and sometimes hot flashes in the pill-free week can all appear. Log them. If they're building, even with a regular bleed, perimenopause is still worth raising.

## 10 · menopause-is-one-day
**Menopause is one day**

Technically, menopause is a single point: the day that marks 12 months since your last period. Everything before it is perimenopause. Everything after it is postmenopause.

You only know you've reached it looking back. The average age is around 51, and perimenopause often runs for 4 years or more before it, sometimes much longer. Most of the symptoms people call "menopause" actually happen during perimenopause, when hormones are swinging most.

That's why many women in their forties are told they're too young for this. They're not too young for perimenopause. Knowing the difference in words gives you a clearer way to describe where you are: not "menopausal", but in the transition.

## 11 · what-to-log-about-your-period
**What to log about your period**

Your cycle is the clearest evidence you have of where you are in the transition. A few details make it useful.

The most important is the first day of each period — that's how cycle length is measured. Beyond that: how many days you bled, how heavy it was, any bleeding between periods, and pain. Logging symptoms on the same days shows whether they cluster around your cycle, which is one of the strongest signs of a hormonal cause.

3 months of this turns "my periods have been weird" into "my cycle has gone from 28 days to 21, the bleeding is heavier, and my sleep and mood are worst in the week before". That's what a doctor needs to hear.

## 12 · before-40-is-different
**When it starts before 40**

If your periods become irregular or stop, with menopause symptoms, before the age of 40, that needs investigating. It is called premature ovarian insufficiency, and it affects about 1 in 100 women.

It isn't simply early perimenopause. The ovaries stop working normally much sooner than expected, and the years without estrogen that follow carry higher risks for bones and heart. Menopause between 40 and 45 is called early menopause and also deserves attention.

Unlike perimenopause after 45, this is diagnosed with blood tests, repeated over several weeks. Hormone treatment is usually recommended until at least the average age of menopause, to protect long-term health. If this sounds like you, ask for tests specifically.

## 13 · fibroids-and-polyps
**Fibroids and polyps**

Fibroids and polyps are both common in your forties, and both can cause heavier, longer or more painful periods. They are usually harmless, and they're treatable.

Fibroids are growths in the muscle wall of the womb. Polyps are small growths in the lining. Both respond to estrogen, and perimenopause, with its higher estrogen peaks, can make them grow or cause more symptoms. Many women have them without knowing.

If your periods have become much heavier or more painful, an ultrasound can usually show whether either is involved. That matters, because it changes the treatment. Heavy bleeding isn't always just hormones, and finding out which it is means you get the right fix.

---

## 14 · bleeding-that-always-needs-checking
**Bleeding that always needs checking**

Most bleeding changes in perimenopause are hormonal. A few patterns should always be looked at by a doctor, however likely a harmless explanation is.

Any bleeding after 12 months without a period. Bleeding after sex. Bleeding between periods that keeps happening. Periods that are suddenly much heavier, or last much longer than they used to. In most cases the cause turns out to be benign — polyps, fibroids, or hormone swings. But these are also the signs of problems in the womb lining or cervix that are best found early.

This is not meant to frighten you. It's so that you know which changes you can log and watch, and which ones you shouldn't wait on. For these, make the appointment.

## 15 · hormone-therapy-with-periods
**Hormone therapy while you still have periods**

You don't have to wait until your periods stop to start hormone therapy. It can be used during perimenopause, when symptoms are often at their worst.

If you still have a womb, hormone therapy includes estrogen for symptoms and a progestogen to protect the womb lining. In perimenopause, this is often given in a cycle that produces a regular bleed. A hormonal coil can provide the progestogen part — and it also treats heavy periods and works as contraception.

Your regimen may need adjusting as your own hormones continue to change. That is normal. Bring your cycle log to the appointment. Knowing your bleeding pattern helps your doctor choose the right approach from the start.
