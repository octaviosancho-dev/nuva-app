# Insights — batch 3: sleep

Category: `sleep`. 15 insights. Three paragraphs each, never more.

## 1 · why-perimenopause-breaks-sleep
**Why perimenopause breaks your sleep**

Sleep problems are one of the most common symptoms of perimenopause, and they often start before anything else. Both of the hormones that protected your sleep are moving.

Progesterone has a sedating effect — it helps you fall asleep and stay there. In perimenopause it drops, and in some cycles barely appears. Estrogen helps regulate body temperature overnight and supports the brain chemicals involved in sleep. When it swings, nights get lighter and more broken, with or without sweats.

If you slept well for decades and then stopped, with no obvious reason, this is the most likely explanation. It is not stress you haven't noticed. Log how you slept every day, even when it was fine. The good nights make the bad ones readable.

## 2 · the-3am-wake-up
**The 3am wake-up has a mechanism**

Waking at 3 or 4am, wide awake, and not getting back to sleep is one of the signature patterns of perimenopause. It isn't random.

In the second half of the night, sleep naturally gets lighter and cortisol, the alertness hormone, starts rising to prepare you for morning. With less progesterone holding sleep together, and an unstable thermostat that can set off a night sweat, that lighter stretch is where you surface. Once you're awake, rising cortisol makes it hard to go back down.

Log the time you wake, not only that you did. The same hour, night after night, is a recognisable pattern, and it is one of the clearest things you can show a doctor to separate hormonal sleep problems from ordinary insomnia.

## 3 · insomnia-without-worry
**When you can't sleep and you're not worried**

Insomnia in perimenopause often doesn't feel like the insomnia people describe. You're not lying awake anxious. You're just awake.

Falling hormone levels change the structure of sleep itself: less deep sleep, more light sleep, more brief awakenings. The brain comes up close to the surface many times a night, and sometimes it doesn't go back down. There's no thought keeping you there — the system that held you under has less to work with.

This matters because the usual advice assumes the problem is a busy mind. If yours isn't, say so. "I'm not stressed, I just wake up and can't get back to sleep" is a clear description of a hormonal pattern.

## 4 · unrefreshing-sleep
**8 hours in bed is not 8 hours of sleep**

You were in bed long enough. You wake exhausted anyway. Unrefreshing sleep is usually fragmented sleep — broken into pieces you don't remember.

Brief awakenings lasting a few seconds don't register in memory, but they pull you out of deep sleep. Night sweats, hot flashes you sleep through, and the lighter sleep of perimenopause all cause them. You can have dozens in a night and swear you slept straight through. The repair work that happens in deep sleep keeps getting interrupted.

Log how rested you feel, not just how long you slept. If you're exhausted after a full night on a regular basis, that is a symptom, not a personal failing — and it can also point to sleep apnoea, which is worth ruling out.

## 5 · vivid-dreams
**Why you're remembering more dreams**

More vivid dreams, or dreams you remember in detail, often come with perimenopause. The simplest reason is that you're waking up more.

You dream most in the stage of sleep called REM, which gets longer toward morning. Dreams are normally forgotten because you sleep through the end of them. When hormone changes and night sweats wake you more often — especially in the early hours — you surface in the middle of a dream and remember it.

So vivid dreams are often a sign of broken sleep, not a separate issue. Log them alongside how many times you woke. Some medications, including some antidepressants, also change dreams, so mention any new ones if the dreams started after them.

## 6 · restless-legs
**Restless legs, and the iron connection**

The urge to move your legs at night, with a crawling or aching feeling that only eases when you move, is called restless legs. It is more common in women, and low iron is one of its best-known causes.

Heavy periods are common in perimenopause, and heavy periods drain iron stores. You can be low in stored iron without being anaemic, and a routine blood count can look normal. Restless legs can appear or get worse as iron runs down.

Ask for a ferritin test — it measures stored iron, not just the iron in your blood. If your periods have become heavy and your legs won't settle at night, those symptoms belong in the same conversation.

## 7 · sleep-apnoea-in-women
**Sleep apnoea looks different in women**

The risk of sleep apnoea — where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep — rises for women around the menopause. It often goes unnoticed, because it doesn't look like the loud snoring people expect.

Progesterone helps keep the airway working during sleep. As it falls, the airway can relax more, and breathing becomes less stable. In women, sleep apnoea often shows up as insomnia, morning headaches, waking unrefreshed, low mood and daytime exhaustion rather than dramatic snoring.

If you are exhausted despite enough time in bed, wake with headaches, or have been told you stop breathing in your sleep, ask about it directly. It is diagnosed with a sleep study, often done at home, and it is very treatable.

## 8 · cbt-for-insomnia
**The most effective treatment for insomnia isn't a pill**

Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, called CBT-I, is the recommended first treatment for long-term insomnia, and it works for women in midlife.

It isn't talking about your feelings. It is a structured programme, usually 4 to 8 weeks, that retrains how your brain links bed with sleep: setting a fixed wake time, limiting time awake in bed, and changing the habits and thoughts that keep insomnia going. It is available in person, by phone, and through apps with a clinical evidence base.

It won't stop night sweats. But once sleep has been broken for months, insomnia can carry on by itself even after the sweats ease. CBT-I treats that part. Ask for it by name.

## 9 · fix-the-heat-first
**When night sweats are the problem, treat the sweats**

If you wake because you're hot, the most direct fix for your sleep is treating the night sweats, not the sleep.

Sleep hygiene advice — no screens, a routine, a dark room — is sensible, but it doesn't touch a thermostat that fires at 3am. Treatments that reduce vasomotor symptoms, including hormone therapy and some non-hormonal medicines, often improve sleep because they remove the thing waking you.

Log your sweats and your sleep on the same day. If your worst nights are sweat nights, that tells you where to aim. It also gives your doctor a reason to treat the sweats, instead of prescribing something that only makes you drowsy.

## 10 · lying-awake-makes-it-worse
**Why lying awake makes it worse**

When you can't sleep, staying in bed and trying harder is the natural response. It is also one of the things that keeps insomnia going.

The brain learns by association. Night after night of lying awake, frustrated, teaches it that bed is a place for being alert. After a while, getting into bed can itself wake you up. The effort to sleep makes you more awake, not less.

If you've been awake for what feels like 20 minutes, get up. Go somewhere dim and do something quiet and dull until you feel sleepy, then go back. It feels backwards, but it is one of the core methods in CBT-I, and it is how the association gets rebuilt.

## 11 · caffeine-timing
**The afternoon coffee is still in your system**

Caffeine lasts longer than it feels like it does. Half of it can still be active 5 to 6 hours after you drink it, and some of it for much longer.

That means a coffee at 3pm is still working at 9pm. You may fall asleep fine, but caffeine makes sleep lighter and reduces deep sleep, which is exactly the kind of sleep perimenopause is already taking from you. Sensitivity can also change over time, so what was fine at 30 may not be at 44.

You don't need to give it up. Try moving your last caffeine to before midday for 2 weeks and log your nights. If your sleep improves, you have your answer from your own data.

## 12 · sleep-and-your-cycle
**Why sleep gets worse before your period**

Sleep often gets worse in the days before your period. That is when progesterone falls, and progesterone is one of the things holding your sleep together.

In perimenopause, the drop can be sharper, and night sweats often cluster in those same days because estrogen falls at the same time. The result is a predictable stretch of bad nights each cycle, followed by relief once bleeding starts.

If your bad nights come in runs, check them against your period days. A cyclical pattern of poor sleep is strong evidence that the cause is hormonal. It also helps you plan — knowing a rough week is coming makes it less of a shock when it arrives.

## 13 · sleep-and-appetite
**Short sleep makes you hungrier**

After a bad night, you're hungrier, and you want sugar and carbohydrates more. That isn't weak willpower. Sleep loss changes the hormones that control appetite.

Short or broken sleep raises ghrelin, which makes you hungry, and lowers leptin, which tells you you're full. It also changes how the brain responds to food, making high-energy food more rewarding. Over weeks of broken sleep, that alone can shift your weight.

This is one reason weight changes in perimenopause. It isn't only metabolism — it's also sleep. When you log a bad night, notice how the next day's eating goes. Improving sleep is one of the few things that helps both at once.

## 14 · what-to-log-about-a-bad-night
**What to log about a bad night**

"I sleep badly" is easy to dismiss. A few specific details turn it into evidence.

The useful details are: how long it took to fall asleep, how many times you woke, what time you woke for good, whether you were hot or sweating, and how you felt the next day. You don't need all of them every night. The time you woke and whether you were sweating are the two that matter most in perimenopause.

After 2 or 3 weeks, those details show a shape: which nights are worst, whether sweats are involved, and whether it tracks your cycle. That is the difference between "have you tried a bedtime routine" and a real conversation about treatment.

## 15 · what-treatment-does-for-sleep
**What treatment does for sleep**

Poor sleep in perimenopause responds to treatment. The right one depends on what is breaking it.

If night sweats are waking you, treating them — with hormone therapy or a non-hormonal medicine — often improves sleep directly. Progesterone, when it is part of hormone therapy, has a mild sedating effect for some women and is often taken at night for that reason. If the insomnia has taken on a life of its own, CBT-I is the most effective long-term treatment. If sleep apnoea is involved, it needs its own treatment.

The thread through all of it is the same: sleep problems in your forties are not something to accept. Bring your log. Knowing which pattern you have is how you get the treatment that fits it.
